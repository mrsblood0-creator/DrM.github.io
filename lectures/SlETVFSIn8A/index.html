Sinus bradycardia

Sinus bradycardia is a sinus rhythm starting from the SA node but with a reduced rate of electrical discharge, resulting in reduced heart rate (bradycardia) which is lower then 60 beats per min

The ECG characteristics of sinus bradycardia are

normal sinus P waves and PR intervals

Regular rhythm

1:1 AV conduction

Rate: < 60 beats/min

Causes

Sinus bradycardia occur in response to three categories of stimuli:

Physiologic: due to vagal tone or in healthy people and athletes due to higher cardiac stroke volume requiring less heart rate to achieve perfusion

Pharmacologic: calcium channel blockers, β-blockers, or digoxin, quinidine, adenosine, class I antiarrhythmic agents, ivabradine, clonidine, reserpine, cimetidine, lithium, amitriptyline

Pathologic: acute inferior myocardial infarction (MI), increased intracranial pressure, carotid sinus hypersensitivity, sick sinus syndrome, eating disorders (such as anorexia nervosa), rhodotoxin poisoning, hypothermia, hypothyroidism, infections (such as diphtheria, acute rheumatic fever, or viral myocarditis), sleep apnea and many more …

Signs and symptoms

decreased heart rate => decreased cardiac output resulting in symptoms such:

Lightheadedness

dizziness

hypotension

Vertigo

syncope

The slow heart rate may also lead to atrial, junctional, or ventricular ectopic rhythms

Treatment

Sinus bradycardia does require treatment when the heart rate is slower than 50 beats/min and there is evidence of hypoperfusion.

Lines of treatment include:

  • Transcutaneous cardiac pacing in unstable cases
  • Atropine
  • Epinephrine, dopamine
  • Transvenous pacing in recurrent cases
  • Glucagon in beta blocker or CCB toxicity

Transcutaneous cardiac pacing is the first line treatment for unstable patients with bradycardia.

Patient is attached to the monitor using external pacing pads, the first pad is placed over the left lateral precordium and the second pad is placed at the level of the heart in the right infra scapular area.

Slowly increase the pacing output from 0 mA to the lowest point where capture is observed, usually at 50 to 100 mA, but may be up to 200 mA. A widened QRS after each pacing spike denotes electrical capture.

If needed, administer a sedative, such as lorazepam, 1 to 2 mg IV, or an opiate, such as morphine, 2 to 4 mg IV, for pain control.

Atropine also can be used

The dose is 0.5 mg IV push, repeated every 3 to 5 minutes as needed up to a total of 3 mg IV.

If given via endotracheal tube, increase the dose by 2 to 2.5 times over the IV dose.

Slow administration or lower doses may cause paradoxical bradycardia.

Atropine may not be effective in cardiac transplant patients since the heart is denervated and has no vagal stimulation.

Epinephrine, 2 to 10 μg/min IV, or dopamine, 3 to 10 μg/kg/min IV, may be used if transcutaneous pacing is not available.

Transvenous pacing is indicated in the patient with symptomatic recurrent or persistent sinus bradycardia due to sick sinus syndrome, second degree Mobitz type II AV block or third degree AV block.

In B blocker or CCB toxicity, Glucagon can be used, dosing is 3 to 10 mg IV over 1 to 2 minutes, followed by an infusion of 1 to 5 mg/h
