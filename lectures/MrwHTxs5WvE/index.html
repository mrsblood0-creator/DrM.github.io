Definition and etiology

  • Inflammation of the appendix, acute abdomen, very common
  • Inflammation then obstruction: most of the cases. Infection with E coli (aerobic) and bacteroides fragilis (unaerobic) mostly
  • Inflammation without obstruction
  • Obstruction then inflammation: Pediatric: Luminal obstruction that occurs as a result of lymphoid hyperplasia. Adults: luminal obstruction due to fecaliths, fibrosis, foreign bodies (food, parasites, calculi) or neoplasia
  • Luminal obstruction leads to bacterial overgrowth and inflammation and continued mucus secretion and exudation which leads to increased intraluminal pressure and referred visceral pain in periumbilical region, resolution can occur in this point but if not increased intraluminal pressure leads to impaired venous drainage and mucosal ischemia leading to gangrene and peritonitis

Patient presentation — history

  • Anorexia first symptom in 95% of patients
  • Visceral-somatic pain sequence: poorly localized abdominal pain that shifts to right iliac fossa after 4–6 hours
  • Vomiting once or twice
  • Nausea
  • Low grade pyrexia (37.2–37.7) start after 6 hours of the onset of the abdominal pain
  • In adult female you want to make sure that you put on your DDx ovarian torsion/cyst, ectopic pregnancy, PID

Patient presentation — examination

  • Localized abdominal tenderness especially in the McBurney’s point (2/3 the distance between umbilicus and the ASIS)
  • Muscle guarding
  • Rebound tenderness
  • Pointing sign (patient pointing where pain began and where it moved to)
  • Rovsing’s sign (deep palpation in left iliac fossa cause pain in right iliac fossa)
  • Psoas sign (patient lie on their left side and examiner extend their right thigh => if patient feel pain then sign is positive and indicate inflamed retrocecal appendix)
  • Obturator sign (patient flex their right knee and examiner slowly internally rotate the right hip => if patient fell pain then sign is positive and indicate inflamed pelvic appendix)

Patient presentation — pregnancy and young children

  • Pregnant females: Diagnosis difficult and delayed because early features maybe attributed to pregnancy; upward displacement of the caecum will change the pain location to RUQ
  • Treatment is operative as perforation carries high maternal and fetal mortality
  • Children less than 3 years of age: Rare but carries higher morbidity and mortality because patient can’t give history => diagnosis to be delayed; diffuse peritonitis develop rapidly because of underdeveloped greater omentum

Patient presentation — elderly and obese

  • Elderly: Higher mortality because lax abdominal wall or obesity lead to delayed diagnosis of generalized peritonitis; atrophied omentum lead to high risk of generalized peritonitis; comorbidities
  • Obese: Delay in diagnosis because obesity mask all local signs of acute appendicitis

Diagnosis

  • Essentially clinical
  • CBC (elevation of WBC)
  • Urinalysis to exclude urinary DDxs
  • Pregnancy test to exclude pregnancy and ectopic pregnancy in females with child bearing age
  • Imaging will show inflammation of appendix
  • US: no radiation, more available, hard to use if in patients with peritonitis
  • MRI: no radiation, expensive, useful for pregnant women and pediatric patients
  • CT scan: high radiation, more specific than US
  • If the findings are classic then no need for imaging

Alvardo score

  • Alvardo score helps you in diagnosing Acute Appendicitis
  • History: Migratory RIF pain — 1; Anorexia — 1; Nausea or vomiting — 1
  • Examination: Right iliac fossa tenderness — 2; Rebound tenderness — 1; Fever — 1
  • Laboratory: Leukocytosis — 2; Shift to left — 1
  • Total — 10

Alvardo score interpretation

  • Score 1–4: low risk appendicitis => check your differential diagnoses
  • Score 5–6: equivocal, Send for imaging (US, CT scan, MRI)
  • 7 and more: strongly predictive for acute appendicitis
  • For Alvardo score of 5 or higher obtain early surgical consultation before sending to imaging

Treatment

  • Surgical: Pre OP preparations: IV fluids; Antibiotics
  • Generally to cover enteric content (gram negatives and anaerobes)
  • Ciprofloxacin/metronidazole, 2nd or 3rd generation cephalosporin can be used instead of ciprofloxacin
  • Non surgical if there is no complications and not preferred in general because of recurrence
  • Bowel rest
  • IV antibiotics
