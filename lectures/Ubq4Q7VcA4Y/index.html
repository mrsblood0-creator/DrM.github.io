Overview

  • Trade name: Depakene
  • The greatest antiepileptic drug to be discovered because it treated most types of seizures
  • Valproic acid commonly mixed with sodium valproate salt to improve GI tolerance of the medications and the result called divalproex sodium
  • It was first made in 1881 and came into medical use in 1962 after it was discovered by accident that it has an antiepileptic effect

Mechanism of action

  • Valproate mechanism of action is not well understood but possible theory may include:
  • Inhibition of voltage gated sodium channels
  • Inhibition of GABA transaminase leading to less breakdown of GABA thus more neuronal inhibition
  • Enhancement of GABA synthesis
  • Modulation of T type Calcium channels
  • Variety of mechanisms gives this medication a broad spectrum of activity against seizures

Therapeutic Uses

  • Broad spectrum antiepileptic drug work on most types of seizures
  • It can be used for myoclonic, atonic, generalized onset tonic clonic seizures and absence seizures
  • it is also effective in focal seizures but not as effective as carbamazepine or phenytoin
  • IV formulations used for status epilepticus
  • It is also used as a mood stabilizer for bipolar disorder
  • It is used for migraine prophylaxis
  • Diabetic neuropathy
  • Schizophrenia

Adverse Effects

  • Some adverse effect usually occur at the beginning of valproate treatment and they include: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, heartburn, thrombocytopenia, tremor, insomnia, depression
  • Weight gain, increase appetite and hair loss are common
  • It also may lead to severe adverse effects such as:
  • Hepatotoxicity
  • Steven Johnson syndrome
  • Psychosis
  • Anaphylaxis
  • SIADH
  • Pancreatitis
  • Aplastic anemia
  • Pregnancy: teratogenic lead to orofacial, cardiovascular anomalies and neural tube defects (spina bifida) and the risk for these anomalies are higher with valproate in comparison with other antiepileptics

Pharmacokinetics

  • Available as oral and IV formulations
  • Oral formula is well absorbed with 85 % bioavailability, food delays absorption but drug more tolerated if given after meals
  • Distribution: valproate is highly bound to plasma proteins by 90%, Vd is 0.4 L/kg
  • Valproate is extensively metabolized by the liver through oxidation, glucuronidation and conjugation
  • Valproate is a weak inhibitor of CYP2C9, UGT, and epoxide hydrolase systems
  • Valproate eliminated through urine and its Half life is between 10- 15 hours

Dosing

  • Initially start with 15 mg/kg/day with slow titration, you can titrate up to 60 mg/kg/day depending on the response
  • Therapeutic levels are between 50 – 150 mcg/ml

Drug Interactions

  • Valproic acid inhibits the metabolism of phenobarbital and ethosuximide leading to higher levels of these medications, higher levels of phenobarbital may lead to coma and respiratory depression
  • Valproic acid displaces phenytoin from plasma proteins and this may lead to phenytoin toxicity
  • It also decrease the clearance of lamotrigine leading to prolongation of lamotrigine half life

Contraindications

  • Significant hepatic impairment (baseline LFTs is mandatory to check for that)
  • Hypersensitivity reactions to the drug
  • Pregnancy

Valproic acid toxicity

  • Acute valproate toxicity manifest as
  • Central nervous system depression in form of drowsiness, confusion, ataxia, nystagmus, diplopia, dysarthria, tremor and coma
  • Metabolic acidosis
  • Hypernatremia
  • Hypoglycemia
  • Hyperammonemia
  • Hepatotoxicity
  • Pancreatitis
  • Multiorgan failure
  • Management
  • Stop valproate
  • Supportive care include fluid therapy, monitoring vital signs and maintaining airway patency
  • Enhancement of elimination by hemodialysis is an option
