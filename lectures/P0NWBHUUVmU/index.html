Why NSAIDs can’t be given in bronchial asthma

Because NSAIDs inhibit COX enzyme but not lipoxygenase enzyme leading to accumulation of leukotrienes which are potent bronchoconstrictors

Aspirin induced asthma

Corticosteroids

Corticosteroids work by inhibiting phospholipase A2 enzyme leading to less arachidonic acid and less synthesis of prostaglandins and leukotrienes which ultimately lead to anti inflammatory effect

Mechanism of action

Corticosteroids can effectively decrease bronchial inflammation and hyperresponsiveness through:

They inhibit B cell function => decrease antibody release and subsequently decrease antigen-antibody reaction

They inhibit T cell function => decrease mediators and cytokine release

They inhibit macrophage activity and stabilize lysosomal membranes => less inflammation

They inhibit mast cells => decrease histamine release and lowers capillary permeability

Corticosteroids cause up regulation of B2 receptors because it lowers inflammation => better response to b2 agonists medications

Their onset of action is gradual and may take anywhere from several days to several weeks for maximal benefit with consistent use

Pharmacokinetics

There is oral, IV and inhaled formulations

Inhaled formulations include liquid capsule formulation given through nebulizer, metered dose inhalers and dry powder inhalers

Widely used ICS include budesonide, fluticasone and beclomethasone

Use in asthma

In acute severe asthmatic attack: IV hydrocortisone

Chronic asthma

ICS: Inhaled corticosteroids (e.g. beclomethasone): FDA indicated treatment of choice for preventing asthma exacerbations in patient with persistent asthma; regular use of these medications reduce frequency of asthma symptoms, bronchial hyperresponsiveness and risk of serious exacerbations; they have minimal systemic side effects, but an appropriate inhalation technique is critical for success of therapy

Oral prednisolone or dexamethasone

Adverse effects

Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) are associated with much less systemic side effects compared to oral and IV corticosteroids, but it may lead to some local side effects:

Dysphonia in 50% of the patients due to myopathy of laryngeal muscles an mucosal irritation

Oropharyngeal candidiasis due to corticosteroid decreasing mouth immunity; this can be avoided by rinsing the mouth after each inhalation and can be treated by nystatin mouthwash or miconazole

Reduction in growth velocity in children using them, these effects in low doses are small and reversible but in higher doses they are significant

ICS may rarely lead to cataract, glaucoma, hypothalamic pituitary adrenal axis dysfunction and impaired glucose metabolism

Oral and IV corticosteroids lead to systemic adverse effects, including:

Osteoporosis

Myopathy

Higher glucose levels

Cushingoid features (truncal obesity, buffalo hump and moon face)

Suppression of HPA

Impairment of growth in children

Infections

Hypertension, edema, arrhythmias

Ecchymosis, skin thinning, stria and impaired wound healing

Precautions

Oral or IV corticosteroids if used for a long period of time, must be withdrawn gradually to avoid acute Addisonian crisis

Corticosteroid lowers potassium levels so Diet should be rich in K

Continuous check for any increase in weight, edema, sugar in urine or BP

If the patient develops acute infection, they must be treated by adequate antibiotics with decreased dose of steroids
