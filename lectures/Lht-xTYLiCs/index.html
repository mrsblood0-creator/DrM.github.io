Rapid sequence intubation

  • RSI include simultaneous administration of an induction agent and a neuromuscular blocking agent to facilitate orotracheal intubation

Rapid sequence intubation (RSI) is a technique indicated when rapid control of the airway is required in cases of:

  • Acute respiratory failure due to inadequate oxygenation or ventilation
  • Airway protection in a patient with an altered mental status.
  • Acute upper gastrointestinal bleed with a high risk of aspiration

Preparation

  • Airway risk assessment before the intervention
  • Positioning of the patient
  • Anatomy
  • ETT tube size
  • Blade selection
  • All necessary supplies must be available at the bedside, including alternative options to secure the airway in case of failure.
  • A checklist is very helpful

Assessing Airway Risk

  • Airway risk assessment is done to determine if an airway will be difficult before the start of the procedure. Multiple mnemonics can be used, a popular one is LEMON
  • L: Look externally (facial trauma, beard, loose teeth, dentures, large incisors); these factors can indicate a difficult airway.
  • E: Evaluate the 3-3-2 rule
  • Can 3 fingers fit between the incisors?
  • Can 3 fingers fit between the tip of the chin and the hyoid bone?
  • Can 2 fingers fit from the top of the thyroid notch to the hyoid bone?
  • A decrease in these distances can be an indication of a difficult airway.
  • M: Mallampati score; assess how much of the posterior pharynx and uvula can be seen (a higher Mallampati score indicates a more difficult airway).
  • Mallampati 1 score: Indicates that the entirety of the uvula can be visualized
  • Mallampati 2 score: Indicates that half of the uvula can be visualized
  • Mallampati 3 score: Indicates that only the base of the uvula can be visualized
  • Mallampati 4 score: Indicates that the uvula cannot be visualized
  • O: Obstruction or obesity
  • obesity (body mass index >30) can predict a difficult airway.
  • Obstruction of the airway, including head and neck cancer and epiglottitis, can indicate a difficult airway.
  • N: Neck mobility
  • assess the neck's mobility; a neck that cannot be maneuvered into an ideal position due to trauma, a C-collar, or rigidity can lead to a more difficult airway

Positioning

  • The neck should be flexed at the lower cervical spine (C6-C7) and extended at the upper cervical spine (C1-C2) "sniffing position"
  • This allows for the best visualization of the anatomy, including the epiglottis and vocal cords.

Anatomy

  • The epiglottis serves as the primary landmark that will direct successful endotracheal intubation.
  • The origin of the epiglottis can be found at the base of the tongue.
  • The valley between the tongue and the epiglottis is called the vallecula.
  • When intubating with the laryngoscope, the tip of the blade should ideally be placed at the base of the tongue in the vallecula, and a ventral and inferior movement of the blade reveals the vocal cords.

Modified Cormack- Lehane

  • The modified Cormack and Lehane Grading system allows the physician to evaluate their view of the vocal cord and estimate the chance of successful intubation.
  • The modified Cormack and lehane grading system consist of 4 grades

Endotracheal Tube Size

For adults:

  • Female: 7.0- to 7.5-mm internal diameter tube
  • Male: 7.5- to 8.0-mm tube

For pediatrics:

  • For uncuffed tubes: (age in years/4) + 4 mm
  • For cuffed tubes: (age in years/4) + 3.5 mm

Blade Selection

  • The most commonly used laryngoscope blades are the Macintosh and Miller blades.
  • The Macintosh blade has a curved spatula that should be placed inside the vallecula and it is used to approximate the curvature of the tongue and move the epiglottis, mandible, hyoid bone, and other soft tissues out of the view of the glottis
  • Multiple sizes available
  • The Miller blade is Flat spatula designed to go over the epiglottis to reveal the vocal cords.
  • Multiple sizes available

Induction Agents

Induction agent administred via rapid IV push to induce anesthesia (uncsconsiosness) , options include:

  • Etomidate: 0.3 mg/kg , it has minimal effect on CVS and RS systems, it decreases ICP
  • Ketamine: 1-2 mg/kg, it increases heart rate and BP, more suitable in trauma, it lead to bronchodiltation, it increases ICP
  • Propofol: 0.5- 1.5 mg/kg, it decreases heart rate and BP, it causes the most respiratory depression
  • Anesthesia is maintained using inhaled volatile liquids, options: sevoflurane, desflurane, isoflurane

Paralytic agents

  • After administration of induction agent, paralytic agent should be administered

Options include:

  • Succinylcholine: 1 -2 mg/kg of total body weight, ultra rapid onset and offset (ideal for RSI), but very risky in patients with hyperkalemia (in burns, trauma, NMS disease), may cause malignant hyperthermia, bradycardia and increased ICP
  • Rocuronium: 1- 1.5 mg/kg of total body weight, onset is a little bit delayed (60 seconds) but it has minimal CVS side effects, no risk of malignant hyperthermia and much more safe in general

Technique

  • Patient, supplies, and team must be properly prepared
  • Induction agents can be administered to the patient first, followed by paralytic agents
  • The bag valve mask should be utilized to pre-oxygenate the patient as much as possible with 100% oxygen and NIPPV.
  • After allowing the paralytic agents to take effect after 60 seconds, place patient in sniffing position and open the mouth with the scissoring technique. The scissoring technique involves placing the right thumb on the right middle finger and inserting the right hand into the patient's mouth. The clinician then "scissors" the patient's mouth open, crossing the thumb and middle finger. This is explained in a video linked in the description
  • The laryngoscope should then be inserted into the patient's mouth with a sweeping motion from right to left, moving the tongue out of view.
  • The clinician should locate the epiglottis and place the laryngoscope in the vallecula.
  • A ventral and inferior motion should be performed with a rigid wrist without abduction or "rocking" of the wrist.
  • Suctioning can be performed to obtain a better view and clear debris, and pressure can be applied to the thyroid cartilage to manipulate the glottis into view.
  • Once the clinician has located the glottis, the endotracheal tube can be passed through the vocal cords.
  • Once passed, the stilette can be removed from the endotracheal tube.
  • The tube should be advanced based on the Chula formula: endotracheal tube depth=0.1 (height in cm) + 4.
  • This equation will give the depth at which the endotracheal tube should be advanced when measured at the patient's teeth.
  • The cuff should be inflated after the tube is advanced to the proper distance.
  • End-tidal capnography, and bilateral lung sounds should all be used to verify placement initially.
  • A chest X-ray should be performed to assure adequate placement.

Contraindications

  • Absolute contraindications include: complete upper airway obstruction and the loss of facial or oropharyngeal landmarks, which will require a surgical airway to be placed
  • Relative contraindications include:
  • Injuries to the airway
  • Anatomic abnormalities
  • The proficiency of the clinician
  • These relative contraindications can be evaluated using the L.E.M.O.N. mnemonic and Cormack and Lehane Grading system discussed already.
