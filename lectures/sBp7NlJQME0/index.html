Definition

  • Retropharyngeal abscess is a collection of pus located in the prevertebral space behind the pharynx (retropharyngeal) while the lateral pharyngeal abscess is located in the lateral part of the pharynx
  • It can lead to life threatening complications such as airway obstruction or sepsis, so it has to be diagnosed and treated carefully

Etiology

  • Retropharyngeal and lateral pharyngeal infections are most often polymicrobial.
  • The usual pathogens include group A streptococcus, staphylococcus aureus , and oropharyngeal anaerobic bacteria
  • There is increase incidence with methicillin-resistant strains of staphylococcus aureus.
  • Other pathogens can include Hemophilus influenzae, Klebsiella, and Mycobacterium avium.

Pathophysiology

  • The retropharyngeal and the lateral pharyngeal lymph nodes that drain the mucosal surfaces of the upper airway and esophagus are located in the neck within the retropharyngeal space and the lateral pharyngeal space
  • The retropharyngeal space is located between the pharynx and the cervical vertebrae and extending down into the superior mediastinum
  • The lateral pharyngeal space bounded by the pharynx medially, the carotid sheath posteriorly, and the muscles of the styloid process laterally
  • The lymph nodes in these deep neck spaces communicate with each other, and upper respiratory tract infection will infect the lymph nodes, infection spread from one node to the others leading to suppurative adenitis and lymph abscess
  • Infection of the nodes usually occurs as a result of extension from a localized infection of the oropharynx.
  • A retropharyngeal abscess can also result from penetrating trauma to the oropharynx, dental infection, and vertebral osteomyelitis.

Epidemiology

  • Retropharyngeal abscess occurs most commonly in children younger than 4 years of age because the retropharyngeal nodes involute after 5 years of age
  • This means infection in older children and adults is much less common (because there is no lymph nodes to transmit the infection so infection has to come by trauma, dental infection or vertebral osteomyelitis).
  • Abscess formation occurs most commonly in winter and early spring.
  • Males are affected more than females and approximately two-thirds of patients have a history of recent ear, nose, or throat infection.

Clinical features

  • Patients with retropharyngeal abscess classically present with fever, irritability, drooling, and dysphagia, and may have inspiratory stridor.
  • Patients may hold their neck in an unusual position with torticollis, hyperextension, stiffness or the may refuse to move their neck.
  • The verbal child might complain of sore throat and neck pain.
  • Other signs can include muffled voice, respiratory distress, or even obstructive sleep apnea.
  • Physical examination can reveal bulging of the posterior pharyngeal wall, and this is present in <50% of infants with retropharyngeal abscess.
  • Cervical lymphadenopathy may also be present.
  • Lateral pharyngeal abscess commonly presents as fever, dysphagia, and a prominent bulge of the lateral pharyngeal wall, sometimes with medial displacement of the tonsil.

Diagnosis

  • Physical examination of the pharynx may show a retropharyngeal mass.
  • Although palpation commonly will demonstrate fluctuance, this could lead to rupture of the abscess.
  • Lateral neck radiograph performed during inspiration with the neck extended may show increased width or an air–fluid level in the retropharyngeal space.
  • The diagnosis is suggested when the retropharyngeal space at C2-C3 is more than 1/3 vertebral body width or at C4 downward is more than the full width of the vertebral body
  • Deep neck infections can be accurately identified and localized with CT scans, but CT accurately identifies abscess formation in only 63% of patients.
  • CT with contrast medium enhancement can reveal central lucency, ring enhancement, or scalloping of the walls of a lymph node and help differentiate abscess from cellulitis.
  • Scalloping of the abscess wall is thought to be a late finding and predicts abscess formation.
  • Incision and drainage and culture of an abscessed node provides the definitive diagnosis

Differential Diagnosis

  • The differential diagnosis includes acute epiglottitis and foreign body aspiration.
  • In the young child with limited neck mobility, meningitis must also be considered.
  • Other possibilities include lymphoma, hematoma, and vertebral osteomyelitis.

Treatment

  • Immediate airway stabilization is the first priority, Intubate unstable patients before performing CT.
  • Treatment include intravenous antibiotics with or without surgical drainage.
  • Antibiotic choice is controversial because most retropharyngeal abscesses contain mixed flora, A third-generation cephalosporin combined with ampicillin-sulbactam or clindamycin to provide anaerobic coverage is effective. The increasing prevalence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus can influence empiric antibiotic therapy.
  • Studies show that >50% of children with retropharyngeal or lateral pharyngeal abscess as identified by CT can be successfully treated without surgical drainage; the older the child, the more likely it is that antimicrobial treatment alone will be successful.
  • Drainage is necessary in the patient with respiratory distress or failure to improve with intravenous antibiotic treatment.
  • Consider adjunctive treatment with parenteral steroids (e.g., dexamethasone 0.15 to 0.6 mg/kg IV to a maximum of 10 mg) to reduce inflammation and edema.
  • The optimal duration of treatment is unknown, and IV antibiotics are given until the patient has begun to improve, followed by a course of oral antibiotics.

Complications

  • Complications of retropharyngeal or lateral pharyngeal abscess include significant upper airway obstruction, rupture leading to aspiration pneumonia, and extension to the mediastinum.
  • Thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein and erosion of the carotid artery sheath can also occur.

Lemierre disease

  • Another complication is infection of the parapharyngeal space (Lemierre disease), in which infection from the oropharynx extends to cause septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein and embolic abscesses in the lungs.
  • The causative pathogen is Fusobacterium necrophorum, an anaerobic bacterial constituent of the oropharyngeal flora.
  • The typical presentation is that of a previously healthy adolescent or young adult with a history of recent pharyngitis who becomes acutely ill with fever, hypoxia, tachypnea, and respiratory distress.
  • Chest radiography demonstrates multiple cavitary nodules, often bilateral and often accompanied by pleural effusion. Blood culture may be positive.
  • Treatment involves prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy with penicillin or cefoxitin; surgical drainage of extrapulmonary metastatic abscesses may be necessary
