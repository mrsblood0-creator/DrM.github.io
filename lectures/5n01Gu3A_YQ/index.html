Terminology

Focal means when doing a biopsy there is less than 50% of glomeruli involved

Segmental means portion of the glomerulus is involved, others are normal

Glomerulosclerosis: sclerosis means scar

So it means less than 50% of glomeruli are scarred

Overview

FSGS is the most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults (in children most common cause of nephrotic syndrome is the MCD)

FSGS is second most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in children

FSGS is common in people of west African descent (the primary genetic type)

FSGS famous for causing damage to transplanted kidney in less than 24 hours

Minimal change disease and FSGS on the same spectrum of podocyte injury (both affect foot processes): MCD is mild injury, FSGS is severe

Poor prognosis

Etiology

Primary FSGS:

idiopathic

Genetic/ familial

Secondary FSGS

HIV

Sickle cell anemia

Drugs (pamidronate, heroin)

Massive obesity

Renal injury (e.g. renal injury from HUS)

Systemic disease

Pathogenesis

In primary FSGS there is injury to the podocyte due to a circulating toxin

Secondary FSGS

Reduced renal mass, sickle cell and obesity lead to increased glomerular capillary blood pressure lead to hyperfiltration through the glomerulus and subsequent podocyte injury

In HIV, drugs (heroin, pamidronate) there is a direct injury to the podocyte either by the virus or the drug

Renal biopsy

Light microscopy shows segmental sclerosis

Immunofluorescence shows deposition of C3 complement and IgM in area of the scar

Electron microscopy shows effacement of foot processes

Since FSGS is focal, the abnormality may not show on renal biopsy and it would be normal leading to initial diagnosis of MCD

Presentation

Primary FSGS typically present with acute fulminant nephrotic syndrome same with minimal change disease

Secondary FSGS is mostly asymptomatic with nephrotic range proteinuria or below that without hypoalbuminemia or edema

Frothy urine

Pitting edema, worse in lower extremities,

Periorbital edema

Nephrotic syndrome, CC BY-SA 3.0 <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0>, via Wikimedia Commons

Abdominal pain due to ascites, severe hypovolemia, peritonitis, pancreatitis, thrombosis, or steroid induced gastritis

Hypertension

Patients may develop white nails sometimes in bands (Muehrcke's lines) during relapses

Adults may develop xanthomas and xanthelasma because of hyperlipidemia

Diagnosis

Nephrotic range proteinuria, urine dipstick test 3+, 4+

24 hours urine protein is > 3.5g

Urine protein creatinine ratio of more than 3

Comprehensive metabolic panel looking for Hypoalbuminemia: serum albumin < 3

Renal function test: BUN and creatinine looking for renal dysfunction

Serum electrolytes looking for calcium and other electrolytes changes because Total serum calcium and vitamin D decreased due to decrease protein bound calcium and decrease vitamin D binding proteins lost in urine respectively, ionized calcium is usually normal

Mild hyponatremia during relapses

Elevated hemoglobin and hematocrit levels and thrombocytosis are usually found during relapse as result of plasma volume contraction

Confirmatory test is renal biopsy

Treatment of primary FSGS

Primary FSGS may respond to high dose glucocorticoids therapy (0.5-2 mg/kg/day) but the response is not as fast as in MCD

Calcineurin inhibitors, cyclophosphamide and mycophenolate mofetil sometimes used but their efficacy is variable

Progression to CKD is common patients who don’t respond to glucocorticoids and disease recurs after renal transplantation

Treatment of secondary FSGS

Treat secondary causes

Treatment

Supportive therapy

Strict blood pressure control < 130/80 mmHg using ACEs or ARBs

Hyperlipidemia treated with statins

Edema treated with sodium restriction and diuretics

Thrombophilia treated with anticoagulation

Infections treated with antibiotics

Complications

Kidney failure is a common long term complication among the FSGS patients

Untreated cases all progress to end stage kidney disease except for less than 10% of them
