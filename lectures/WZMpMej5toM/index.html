Suturing materials

0:35 in the video

  • Used to hold tissue together while in healing phase
  • Suturing material should be: Sterile; Cause minimal tissue reaction; Strong
  • If the suturing happen in tissues where absorption of the suture material is necessary then the material should be capable to be absorbed by the tissues once healing phase complete

Types of suturing materials

1:35 in the video

  • Absorbable: Natural, Synthetic
  • Non absorbable: Natural, Synthetic

Absorbable materials

2:35 in the video

  • Materials that absorbed by the body by enzymes or hydrolysis
  • Absorption cause these materials to weaken progressively until they get absorbed fully
  • Rate of absorption is the speed by which the body absorb these materials and it is determined by the type of material used and the body part being sutured and the patient factors
  • In optimal situation, absorption process start when the wound get adequate strength
  • Absorbable materials used for suturing deeper layers of the body because suture removal is not possible, and also used for areas that heal rapidly

Types and uses of absorbable materials

4:00 in the video

  • Catgut (natural): Not commonly used
  • Chromic catgut (natural): Closure of mucosal wounds; Not commonly used
  • Polyglactin aka Vicryl (synthetic): Suturing of deep tissues, percutaneous closure; Most frequently used, retains strength for 3 weeks
  • Monocryl (synthetic): Useful as buried suture, retains strength for less than month
  • Polydioxanone aka PDS (synthetic): Useful for wounds that require prolonged dermal support, support longer than Vicryl

Non absorbable materials

6:05 in the video

  • Those materials are not absorbed by the body and they stay in the tissues and they get walled off by the inflammatory process or they are removed manually
  • They preferred when long term tissue support is needed such as in tissues that heal slowly (vascular ligation, anastomoses) and also used for muscles, tendons, fascia and superficial wounds

Classification and uses of non absorbable materials

6:50 in the video

  • Class I: Silk (natural) => used for securing drains
  • Class I: Nylon (synthetic) => skin suturing, buried sutures that require prolonged dermal support
  • Class I: Polypropylene aka prolene (synthetic) => skin sutures, areas with anticipated swelling
  • Class II: Cotton (natural) => not commonly used
  • Class II: Linen (natural) => used generally when tissue support for long time not required
  • Class III: Surgical steel (natural) => orthopedic procedures, skin closure, hernial repair

Another classification of sutures

8:45 in the video

  • Monofilament
  • Multifilament (braided)

Size of suture materials

9:30 in the video

  • Suture materials assigned a number for size in reverse order, this means the higher the number the smaller the size and those have different uses
  • 0-0 and 1-0: Abdominal wall closure and securing drains
  • 2-0 and 3-0: Closure of thick tissues (skin, facia, muscles, tendons)
  • 4-0 and 5-0: Closure of skin (hands, face, feet, pediatric patients)
  • 6-0 and 7-0: Plastic surgery and blood vessels repair
  • 8-0 to 11-0: Ophthalmic surgery and microvascular reconstruction
