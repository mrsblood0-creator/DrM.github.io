Omphalocele

Definition

  • Omphalocele is a congenital abdominal wall defect, in which the abdominal viscera is herniating through the umbilical cord outside meaning the viscera will be covered by a sac (umbilical cord lining)
  • The location of the omphalocele defect could be mid abdominal, hypogastric or epigastric
  • Omphalocele sac is comprised of an inner peritoneal layer and an outer amnion layer with Wharton’s jelly in between
  • Most commonly organs herniating are intestine, stomach and liver

Epidemiology

  • Omphalocele incidence is around 1 in 1500 in 14-18 weeks of gestation and 1 in 5000 at birth (most of omphalocele cases discovered early will be spontaneously lost or the pregnancy is terminated)
  • Incidence of omphalocele remained stable in comparison with gastroschisis incidence which is increasing

Omphalocele has relatively high incidence of other congenital anomalies by 50-70% of the cases, these include:

  • Cardiac anomalies
  • GIT anomalies
  • Exstrophy of the bladder
  • Neural tube defects
  • Lung hypoplasia

Omphalocele can be associated with several syndromes including:

  • Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome
  • Pentalogy of Cantrell
  • Trisomies 13, 18 and 21

Beckwith- Weideman Syndrome

  • Omphalocele
  • Macroglossia
  • Organomegaly
  • Hypoglycemia
  • increased risk of Wilms tumor, hepatoblastoma and neuroblastoma in the future

Pentalogy of Cantrell

  • Omphalocele
  • Diaphragmatic hernia
  • Cardiac anomalies
  • Pericardial defect
  • Sternal cleft

Pathophysiology

  • Pathophysiology of omphalocele is related to embryonic folding
  • Embryonic folding is the process by which the fetus converts from flat sheet of cells, into a tube like structure and it start at 4 weeks
  • Embryonic folding has to be complete for the abdominal wall to close , if not then omphalocele develops
  • If the deficit in the embryonic folding process was related to the cranial fold then the omphalocele would be associated with other cranial fold defects like diaphragmatic hernia, cardiac anomalies, pericardial defect and sternal cleft
  • if the deficit is related to caudal fold then omphalocele might be associated with bladder exstrophy

Causes

  • Cause unknown
  • But there is risk factors

Risk factors

  • Genetic predisposition and chromosomal abnormalities
  • Maternal age of 35 years and older
  • Maternal use of salicylates like aspirin
  • Maternal diabetes
  • Maternal cigarette smoking or alcohol or illicit drug use

Diagnosis

  • Omphalocele can be diagnosed by routine two dimensional US evaluation by 18 weeks of gestation, first trimester detection is possible only with three dimensional US
  • Elevation of maternal serum AFP present in most cases same with gastroschisis
  • Gastroschisis can be differentiated from omphalocele by US too, in gastroschisis there is lack of membranous covering of the bowel
  • In Omphalocele there is high probability of CVS, CNS and genitourinary anomalies, 70% of these anomalies can be detected by US thus knowing the survival rate (survival rate is highly hindered by cardiac anomalies), in comparison to Gastroschisis there is low probability for GIT, CNS, CVS, MSK and genitourinary anomalies

Delivery of Omphalocele baby

  • The goal is to deliver omphalocele patient as close to term as possible to maximize fetal growth and lung development
  • Spontaneous vaginal delivery is the preferred since C section has not shown any advantage
  • BUT many neonates with giant omphaloceles continue to be delivered by C section in many centers worldwide

Resuscitation

  • After delivery of omphalocele newborn, ventilation or intubation might be needed
  • Blood glucose is checked immediately and Dextrose might be given if it was low (Beckwith Wiedemann syndrome)
  • NG decompression tube should be inserted and IV access obtained
  • Thorough search for associated anomalies is important, chest x ray looking for elongated thorax (pulmonary hypoplasia) and echocardiography looking for cardiac anomalies
  • The sac should be covered with saline soaked gauze and dressed to minimize fluid losses
  • Bolus IV normal saline 20 ml/kg after delivery might be needed to substitute the third space fluid loss and insensible fluid losses, fluid and temperature losses are much less than with gastroschisis
  • Initial dose of ampicillin and gentamicin shortly after delivery to prevent GIT infections
  • Parenteral nutrition is very important in omphalocele and should be used until bowel function goes back to normal

Risk assessment

  • Risk assessed based on presence of associated anomalies
  • Isolated omphalocele (no associated anomalies) has the best survival of 90%, presence of associated anomalies decreases survival
  • Omphalocele can be classified into hypogastric, central and epigastric
  • Epigastric has the worst survival rate because of the presence of cardiac anomalies

Treatment

  • Treatment is with surgery goal is to return the intestine back to the abdominal cavity and slowly introduce feeding to the baby
  • Treatment options depend on the size of the omphalocele, baby’s gestational age and presence of associated comorbidities like respiratory insufficiency and associated anomalies

Treatment options

  • Primary closure of the defect: used in small omphaloceles (< 3 cm)
  • Delayed closure: used when the patient has giant omphalocele or has small omphalocele with associated comorbidities
  • Staged closure: used in patients with large defects and no associated comorbidities

Primary closure

  • Immediate primary closure consist of excision of the omphalocele sac, reduction of its content, umbilical cord is dissected and ligated and closure of the fascia and skin over the abdominal contents
  • Primary closure used in small defects < 3 cm in diameter
  • Larger defects that are easy to close (in case of minimal loss in abdominal domain) also can be treated by primary closure in the first month of life

Delayed closure

  • Used when the patient has giant omphalocele or has small omphalocele with associated comorbidities

This method include:

  • Umbilical cord is ligated and excised
  • the defect is then dressed daily with topical sulfasalazine non adherent dressing
  • with time the amnion become epithelized and the defect size decreases
  • In around 1 year when comorbidities have improved and the defect size decreased then definitive closure can be performed

Staged closure

  • Used in patients with large defects and no associated comorbidities
  • When there is significant loss of domain in the abdominal cavity, primary closure is not possible (lead to abdominal compartment syndrome), and that is when the staged closure comes in context
  • Staged closure is done using amniotic sac inversion technique
  • All of the amnion inversion procedure is done extraperitoneal and there is less risk of future adhesions and intestinal obstruction with this method
  • The amnion is inverted by attaching a silastic silo to the amnion-skin junction using sutures
  • The bowel and liver are progressively reduced into the abdomen, the intraabdominal pressure is monitored using NG tube (should not exceed 20 mmHg) to prevent abdominal compartment syndrome
  • Reduction progresses on a daily basis until the contents lays flat and that is when the silo is removed
  • then the amnion is pulled and detachment of intestine is done from the amnion then it is inverted back and the edges are approximated and this process is repeated multiple times until simulated totoal closure of the abdominal wall is possible
  • Finally, The amnion is everted and dissected and abdomen is closed

Complications

  • Early complications include
  • Pulmonary hypoplasia and chronic lung disease and those are due to omphalocele disease process,
  • Cholestasis, sepsis, developmental delay, hypotonia, respiratory insufficiency, renal failure and abdominal compartment syndrome and those come as a result from the treatment
  • Late complications include hernia and GERD due to increase abdominal pressure
