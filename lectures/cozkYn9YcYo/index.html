Epiglottitis

Epiglottitis (Supraglottitis) is acute inflammation of the epiglottis, and it is a medical emergency because there is a risk of sudden airway obstruction and death

Epiglottis is a piece of tissue located at base of the tongue that prevents food from entering the trachea

藤澤孝志, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons · סתו כסלו, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

Etiology

Epiglottitis is rare nowadays, because it is mainly caused by Hemophilus influenzae type b, and vaccination (Hib vaccine) has decreased its occurrence.

Now it is mostly caused Streptococcus and Staphylococcus species and that occur in immunized children. Candida may be a cause in immunocompromised patients.

Clinical features

It is characterized by an acute rapidly progressive course of high fever, sore throat, dyspnea and respiratory distress

The degree of respiratory distress at presentation is variable and lack of respiratory distress at presentation is deceiving and it could be the first manifestation

Hariadhi, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

The classic presentation is usually a healthy child suddenly develops sore throat and fever and within hours, the child appear toxic

Swallowing is difficult, breathing is labored and drooling is present

The child may sit in tripod position, sitting upright and leaning forward with chin up and mouth open while bracing on the arms

Hariadhi, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

A brief period of air hunger and irritability is followed by rapidly increasing cyanosis and coma

Stridor is a late finding and suggest near complete airway obstruction

The barking cough (typical of croup) is rare in Epiglottitis

Usually no other family members are ill with acute respiratory symptoms

Diagnosis

The diagnosis is clinical in patients presenting with the classic presentation

If the diagnosis is uncertain, then lateral neck films should be taken at the bedside in extension and during inspiration with a minimum of disturbance. Thumb sign might be seen

If it is not seen then diagnosis require visualization using laryngoscope in the operating room or in the intensive care unit

Med Chaos, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons

The epiglottis is large, cherry red and swollen in epiglottitis

Anxiety provoking interventions such as phlebotomy, IV placement, placing the child supine or direct inspection of the oral cavity should be avoided until the airway is secure because they may lead to airway compromise

If patient require to be transported, a physician skilled in airway management and intubation should accompany the child at all times

Blood cultures are positive in up to 90% of patients, whereas cultures from the epiglottis are less sensitive.

Treatment

Establish a definitive airway using either endotracheal or nasotracheal intubation or if failed then tracheostomy regardless of the degree of apparent respiratory distress because around 6% of children with epiglottitis without a definitive airway die compared to < 1% of those with a definitive airway

The duration of intubation depends on the clinical course of the patient and duration of epiglottic swelling and extubation is done when swelling subsides, examination is done using direct laryngoscope or flexible fiberoptic laryngoscope

intubation usually last for 2-3 days because response to antibiotics is rapid

During preparation for intubation by gathering the right personnel and equipment

Keep the patient seated and upright. Provide oxygen and administer nebulized racemic or L-epinephrine.

In the event of total airway loss, attempt bag-valve-mask ventilation.

Steroids are used to decrease mucosal edema of the epiglottis. Use methylprednisolone 2 mg/kg IV or dexamethasone 0.15 to 0.6 mg/kg IV.

Administer antibiotics: cefuroxime 50 mg/kg/dose IV, cefotaxime 50 mg/kg/dose IV, or ceftriaxone 50 mg/kg/dose IV are appropriate empiric options. In regions with increased cephalosporin resistance, vancomycin 10 mg/kg/dose IV should be added.
