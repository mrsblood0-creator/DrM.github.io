Overview

Skin grafting or flapping is a type of surgery involves transplantation of skin from the donor site to the recipient site.

Grafting or flapping used in

Extensive wound or trauma

Burns

Areas of extensive skin loss due to infection

Surgeries require skin replacement like removal of skin cancers

Skin graft

Skin graft consist of epidermis and some portion of the dermis that is removed from its blood supply and transferred to another location

Humberto Guerra, Juan Carlos Palomino, Eduardo Falconí, Francisco Bravo, Ninoska Donaires, Eric Van Marck, and Françoise Portaels., Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons

Skin graft uses

Close any wound with good blood supply

Temporary closure (in case of allografts given to patient that keep them covered for 2 weeks and then they automatically shed because of rejection)

Control infection

Close donor site (in case taken flap from a donor and site was large it closed with graft)

Mucosal replacement (urethra, oral cavity)

Skin graft types

  • Autograft => same person
  • Allograft (homograft) => from different human
  • Xenograft (heterograft) => from different species

Graft types according to thickness

Full thickness skin grafts contain epidermis and entire dermis with its elastic fibers => full thickness skin graft have elastic properties and primary contracture which make it good for replacing the sites that need elasticity like anterior elbow joint skin

Partial thickness skin grafts contain epidermis and varying thickness of the dermis and it is associated with secondary contracture

Primary contracture vs secondary contracture of skin graft

Primary contracture is the immediate recoil of freshly harvested grafts as a result of the elastin in the dermis, the more the thickness the more the dermis the more the primary contracture, primary contracture is the good types of contracture making graft more elastic

Secondary contracture involve the contracture of the healed graft due to myofibroblast activity and it is more in the split thickness grafts. Secondary contracture is bad making the graft more prone to hypertrophic scarring

Survival of skin graft

Survival of skin graft depends on the ability of the graft to receive nutrients through revascularization. Revascularization of skin graft occurs in three phases:

Serum imbibition phase which last for the first 24 hours after grafting. when graft placed into the recipient bed binding the graft to the bed, absorption of nutrients into the graft occurs by capillary action from the recipient bed

Inosculation phase for the second 24 hours after grafting. In which vascular network is established between the cut vessels on the underside of the skin graft and the capillary beds in the wound bed

Revascularization phase: in the third 24 hours after grafting. the graft is revascularized through kissing of the capillaries that are already formed in phase 2

Graft failure causes

hematoma under graft => elevation of graft and no kissing of blood vessels

infection

inadequate immobilization => like in anterior elbow you want to put back slap to immobilize elbow for success of graft

Misevaluation of the wound => in bed with poor blood supply or infection

Dependent position

Graft upside down => in thin graft you can't differentiate between up and down

Mesh graft

Graft taken and made into mesh

Used in big defect and limited donor area, also used in high exudate wound, and irregular bed of graft

Flap

Transplantation of tissue with its blood supply

Doctor roach, CC BY-SA 3.0 <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0>, via Wikimedia Commons

Flap uses

Covering recipient beds that have poor vascularity

Reconstructing the full thickness of the eyelids, lips, ears, nose, and cheeks

Padding body prominences like heel

When it is necessary to operate through the wound at a later date to repair underlying structures

Flaps provide functional motor unit

Flaps used in controlling infection to increase blood supply

Flaps types according to location taken from

Local flaps from same site as the defect, and there are many types of them

advancement flaps => moved forward

rotational flaps => rotated circularly

Transpositional flap

Regional flaps from same region as the defect like in arm or hand

Distant flaps is from another region

Taylornate, CC BY-SA 3.0 <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0>, via Wikimedia Commons

Flaps types according to pattern of blood supply

  • Flaps are classified according to blood supply into axial flaps and random flaps:
  • Random flap: random blood supply
  • Axial flap: there is a well named artery in the pedicle of the flap
  • island flap
  • Peninsular flap
  • free flap

Types of flap according to content

  • Skin
  • Muscle
  • Omentum
  • Fascial
  • Chondral
  • Periosteal
  • Mixed

Flap causes of failure

Intrinsic factors

Inadequate blood supply

Arteriovenous shunts

Extrinsic factors

Infection

Hypotension

Compression

Hematoma

Smoking

Monitoring viability of the flap

Monitor by clinical tests, chemical and instrumental methods

Clinical tests

Color and temperature of the flap and area around it

Capillary refiling

Dermal bleeding

Atropine absorption

Histamine test

Chemical method: fluorescein dye injection

Instrumental

Doppler US

Photoplethysmography

Tissue PH, PO2, PCO2
