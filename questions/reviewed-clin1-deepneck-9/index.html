Origin of pulmonary nodules

Choose the single best answer, then reveal the explanation.

A young adult with an infected internal jugular thrombus develops several peripheral pulmonary nodules, some with cavitation. Echocardiography shows no valve vegetation. Which route most directly accounts for spread from the neck to the lungs?

  1. Infected venous emboli reaching the pulmonary arteries through the right heart
  2. Direct contiguous spread through the diaphragm
  3. Systemic arterial emboli originating in the left heart
  4. Aspiration of infected oropharyngeal secretions alone
  5. Spread from a primary pulmonary infection into the neck veins
Reveal answer and explanation
Correct answer

Infected venous emboli reaching the pulmonary arteries through the right heart

Venous fragments enter the right heart and then the pulmonary arterial circulation, producing septic embolic lesions.

Understanding the options

Infected venous emboli reaching the pulmonary arteries through the right heart
Venous fragments enter the right heart and then the pulmonary arterial circulation, producing septic embolic lesions.
Direct contiguous spread through the diaphragm
The neck venous source and scattered peripheral lung nodules support hematogenous embolic spread rather than contiguous diaphragmatic extension.
Systemic arterial emboli originating in the left heart
Left-sided emboli usually enter the systemic arterial circulation, not the pulmonary arteries.
Aspiration of infected oropharyngeal secretions alone
Aspiration can cause lung infection but does not explain the connection between an infected jugular thrombus and embolic nodules.
Spread from a primary pulmonary infection into the neck veins
The clinical sequence identifies the neck infection and jugular thrombosis as the source, not a consequence of lung disease.
