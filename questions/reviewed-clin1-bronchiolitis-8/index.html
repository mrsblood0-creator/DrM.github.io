Avoiding misleading routine imaging

Choose the single best answer, then reveal the explanation.

A 9-month-old has a typical first episode of bronchiolitis with diffuse findings and no focal signs. A trainee proposes a routine chest radiograph to decide whether antibiotics are needed. What is the best reason to avoid that approach?

  1. Nonspecific changes may be mistaken for bacterial pneumonia
  2. A radiograph reliably determines whether RSV or another virus is present
  3. Radiographic severity reliably determines the required antibiotic dose
  4. An abnormal radiograph reliably establishes bacterial coinfection
  5. A normal radiograph safely overrides clinical respiratory distress
Reveal answer and explanation
Correct answer

Nonspecific changes may be mistaken for bacterial pneumonia

Routine imaging rarely changes typical bronchiolitis management and nonspecific abnormalities can prompt inappropriate treatment.

Understanding the options

Nonspecific changes may be mistaken for bacterial pneumonia
Routine imaging rarely changes typical bronchiolitis management and nonspecific abnormalities can prompt inappropriate treatment.
A radiograph reliably determines whether RSV or another virus is present
Radiographs do not identify the specific respiratory virus.
Radiographic severity reliably determines the required antibiotic dose
Antibiotics are not routine bronchiolitis treatment, and radiographic appearances do not set a dose.
An abnormal radiograph reliably establishes bacterial coinfection
Atelectasis and other bronchiolitis changes may mimic pneumonia without bacterial coinfection.
A normal radiograph safely overrides clinical respiratory distress
Clinical severity still determines care even if radiography is normal.
